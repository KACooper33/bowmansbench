## The five things you actually need

**A riser.** The handle the rest of the bow bolts to. Published prices for a full-length ILF riser run from $119.99 to $399.99 on the [riser comparison page](/risers/under-500/), and none of that spread buys you a better bow on its own; it buys features like mounting points for weights.

**Limbs.** The two flexible arms that bend and store energy. Published prices run from $99.99 for a wood-core pair to $149.99 for carbon-foam, on the [limbs comparison page](/limbs/carbon-vs-wood/). A beginner does not need carbon limbs. A wood-core pair does the same job while you are still learning what you want from the sport.

**A tab.** Protects the drawing fingers and, for barebow, carries the marks you use to string walk. Published prices run from $19.99 to $51.05 on the [tabs comparison page](/tabs/best-stringwalking-tabs/).

**A plunger.** A small tuning device that sits behind the arrow rest. It is legal in barebow, and it is easy to assume otherwise if you have read about other no-sight divisions. Published prices run from $39.99 to $199.99 on the [plungers comparison page](/plungers/beiter-vs-shibuya/), and the cheaper of the two is a reasonable first plunger.

**Arrows.** Matched to your riser, limbs and draw length, not bought generically. Published prices for a dozen shafts run from $109.99 to $499.99 on the [arrows comparison page](/arrows/), and the cheapest option there is not a compromise, it is simply aimed at an archer still finding their spine rather than defending a world record.

Add the lowest published price for each of the five together, riser through arrows, and a first setup comes to about $389.95. Add the highest price on each page instead and it comes to about $1,301.01. Most archers starting out land closer to the low end than the high one; the top of that range is a premium riser paired with a premium arrow, not a typical first purchase.

## What barebow actually bans, so you do not buy it by accident

Barebow bans a sight, a clicker, and stabilisers. It does not ban a plunger, and it does not ban weights added to the riser. The [barebow rules guide](/guides/barebow-rules/) covers all of this with the rulebook article numbers, and is worth reading before you buy anything, because the difference between barebow, traditional and Olympic recurve equipment is not obvious from a retailer's category page. The [barebow vs traditional guide](/guides/barebow-vs-traditional/) and the [Olympic recurve vs barebow guide](/guides/olympic-recurve-vs-barebow/) both exist for exactly that confusion.

## What can wait

**Weights.** Useful once your riser, limbs and plunger are set up and you know what balance you are missing. Not needed to shoot your first arrows.

**A second set of limbs.** Buy one set, learn on it, and only add a second poundage once you know you are staying with the sport.

**Anything marketed as premium.** A $499.99 arrow shaft and a $124.99 arrow shaft are both real, published, legal choices, and the expensive one is not the better choice for a first setup. It is the choice for an archer who already knows exactly what they need.

## Who is writing this, and why it might matter to you

This site is written by someone new to archery, learning barebow alongside their family. The full reasoning for that is on the [about page](/about/), but the short version is that a beginner explaining what a beginner needs has one advantage a longtime archer does not: nothing here is written from twenty years of assuming you already know the basics.

Every price on this page is a real, published figure, checked and dated on the page it comes from. Nothing has been estimated to make the numbers round.
